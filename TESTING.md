# How blind proves what it does

blind makes promises about private text, so its claims have to be checkable by someone who doesn't trust us. This file lists every claim and the evidence behind it, and says how the evidence is produced. Nothing here is aspirational. A claim without a passing test doesn't belong in the README.

## The claims table

## 1. Property tests (fast-check)

Generators build realistic emails from templates, with random names (Spanish and English lists), organizations, emails, phones, IBANs, IDs, amounts in EU and US formats, and dates in every supported format and language. Each property runs 2,000 cases in `pnpm test` and 20,000 in `pnpm verify` (`test:deep`), with the seed printed so a failure replays exactly (`SEED=<n> pnpm test test/properties`; `SEED=1547257088` is the seed that timed out at 120 s under load, though on an idle machine it runs no slower than the default). fast-check shrinks a failure to the smallest input that breaks. Generated phones and mailboxes sit in the same reserved ranges and unownable forms as the fixtures (C46), while generated cards, IBANs, ids, IP addresses and free text may fall in live ranges and are never written anywhere: a failure reports the seed, the replay path (`FC_PATH=<path>`, with `-t` to pick the one property) and the kind and length of each input, and the value itself is read by replaying under a debugger.

How long they take. Each property is a test with the `LONG` limit from `test/support/properties.ts`, 600 s at 2,000 cases and scaled up with `NUM_RUNS` so `test:deep` keeps the same margin, instead of the 120 s `testTimeout` in `vitest.config.ts`: at 20,000 cases P3, P5, P6 and P8 take 185 to 320 s each and P10 465 s. Vitest runs test files in parallel and the tests inside one file one after another, so the properties live in `test/properties/`, six files of about equal running time: in a single file they ran for 4 to 9 minutes on a 6-core Intel i7-8700B (12 threads) and 75 minutes at 20,000 cases, and that file alone set the length of `pnpm test` and `test:deep`. Six and not one per property, because a fresh worker pays its own warm-up: on the GitHub runner a property moved into a file of its own took 6 to 25 s longer than inside the shared file (P5 and P8 went from 6 to 27 s). `vitest.config.ts` sets `maxWorkers` to every CPU, since the default keeps one for the main process, and on that runner's two CPUs that left one worker and the files running one after another. On the 12-thread machine `pnpm test` went from 273 to 149 s and `test:deep` from 75 to 22 minutes, under a load average of 4 to 15 from a VM. On the runner the gate's `pnpm test` step went from 5.5 to 7.2 minutes with the split, while the 20,000-case job finished in 37 minutes where the single worker had the five heaviest files still to run after 30 of its 60: every property draws its cases from the same seed, so in one file the later properties reused the regular expressions the earlier ones had compiled from the same values, and each file now compiles its own. One property alone on a core, at the default seed and at 1547257088 with a load average below 6: P1 45 to 71 s, P10 46 to 52 s, P11 44 to 50 s, P13 27 to 53 s, P14 16 to 51 s, P15 24 to 33 s, P17 30 to 40 s, P2, P12 and P18 18 to 31 s, P9 and P20 15 to 17 s, and every other property under 20 s. Under a load of 8 to 10, P1 has taken 120 s and more, so a slower machine or a busy runner should expect two to three times these numbers. The `caseVariants` unit test in `test/pairlog.test.ts` walks every capitalisation of a 10-letter address and the first 20,000 of a 22-letter one, and runs in well under a second.

- **P1 round trip.** `unblind(blind(x).texts[i], mapping) === x[i]` for every text in a multi-text call.
- **P2 no survivors.** Every value the generator inserted is absent from the blind output, as a whole word and case-insensitively.
- **P3 known and deny.** Random `known` and `deny` lists are absent from the output in any case, except a value made only of stop words written in lower case, which C3 leaves as an ordinary word. The report's `unenforced` lists exactly the entries made of stop words (`stop-word`), the full names with a one-letter part (`one-letter-part`) and the other full names with a capitalised part on the stop list (`stop-word-part`). The generated values have no Hangul, so `hangul-inside-word` is covered by the C40 known-value tests instead.
- **P4 relations.** When the generator inserts `base`, `p%` and `base × (1 + p/100)`, the blind copy's values still satisfy the relation to the cent. The same goes for sums and chains like base, VAT, total, and for one amount that is at once a surcharge on one base and a percentage of another, which holds both through the redraw C4 describes.
- **P5 dates.** Every annotated date has a readable replacement in the output that moved by the same `dayShift`, `dayShift % 7 === 0`, and `dayShift !== 0`. The weekday of each date is unchanged, and so is the gap between any two dates. An apparent date inside another annotated value, such as `15 JUNE 1007` inside an IBAN, is not a date to shift. `SEED=711059849 NUM_RUNS=2000` exposed this mistaken expectation.
- **P6 determinism.** Same seed, same output. Over 100 seed pairs, different seeds change at least one surrogate.
- **P7 shape.** A surrogate has the same kind and format as its original: phones keep separators and digit count, IBANs keep country, length and grouping, amounts keep currency, symbol position and decimal style.
- **P8 idempotent leak check.** `assertNoLeaks` passes on every generated case. A case where it would throw is a failure of P2 or P3, never "expected".
- **P9 typed ids.** Valid cards on several networks, SSNs, DNI, NIE and CIF numbers, EU VAT numbers and IPv4 and IPv6 addresses, in the layouts people write them. The fake passes the same validator, keeps its prefix and layout, the same number in a second layout gets the same fake, and the text round trips.
- **P10 conversations.** Two or three emails blinded one after the other, each call given the previous mapping and only the known values it brings in itself. The last turn mentions a person from the first turn bare. Nothing from the first turn may survive, no pair may change, and every turn must unblind with the final mapping.
- **P11 streaming.** A blind text with model-like additions around it, cut into random chunks of 1 to 48 characters, fed to `createUnblinder`. The joined output and the invented list must equal one-shot `unblind`. Disabling the hold-back or the span check makes this property fail, which was checked when it was written.
- **P12 placeholders.** A multi-text call with `replaceWith: 'placeholder'`. Every pair is a placeholder of its own kind, each kind's numbers first appear in the blind texts as 1, 2, 3 in reading order, no value has two placeholders and no placeholder stands for two values, and the mapping records the mode with nothing scaled or shifted. Starting the count at 2 makes it fail.
- **P13 round trip in the other modes.** Placeholder or random, picked per case. Every text of a multi-text call unblinds to itself, and the answer with model-like additions, an unknown placeholder among them, streams in random chunks to exactly the one-shot result and invented list. Restoring placeholders with the case habit of fake names, or dropping the unknown-placeholder check, makes it fail.
- **P14 conversations in the other modes.** Like P10, with the mode named on the first turn only. Every turn must keep the mode, a factor of 1 and a shift of 0, keep every earlier pair in place, never give a value seen before a second fake, and round trip with the last mapping, which `checkMapping` accepts.
- **P15 leak check in the other modes.** Like P2, P3 and P8 together: no inserted or known value survives, the check is clean, a planted deny entry left in the text is reported, and the report holds no value.
- **P16 amounts kept apart.** Amounts in one style, from 1.00 or 1,000 whole units up and at least 10 units apart, many with a neighbour one unit away, plus the sum of two of them written exactly and one unit off, so the relation finder reads several amounts as the same sum. The text round trips, equal amounts keep one fake, different amounts get different fakes, and each fake stays within 5 cents or 0.5% of the real amount times the factor (the rule `scripts/bench/score.ts` scores), or within 3 units or 0.5% for amounts written without cents. Turning off the move to a free fake makes it fail.
- **P17 spellings.** One person, org, role phrase ("la Empresa", "the Company") and address, each written several ways: as given, in capitals, in lower case, without accents, with the first letter changed. A user name is a first name glued to digits and a phone carries an extension glued on with `x`. A stub detector tags the role phrase in any case and the user name, the way a NER model does. In every `replaceWith` mode the text round trips exactly, and in `fake` mode the role phrase keeps one fake in all its letter cases. Restoring a fake through the first pair in any case, or the old rule that a fake may not touch a digit, makes it fail.
- **P18 language.** An English email, a Spanish email, or a thread with an English reply above a quoted Spanish email, with its names, companies and places passed as `known`. Every name word, company, place and private address gets its fake from the lists of the language of its part. Picking from one list of both languages, as blind did before, makes it fail on the second case.
- **P19 gender.** An English or Spanish first name, a woman's or a man's, including women's names that do not end in "a" (Imogen, Rose, Belén, Pilar) and every fake first name written as a real one, with a surname of its language, in a sentence of its language. The fake is a first name of the same gender and language. The old rule, a final "a" plus a short Spanish list, gives Imogen a man's name and makes it fail.
- **P20 mobiles.** UK mobiles (`+44 7700 900xxx`, `0044 7`, `+447`, and `07` in three layouts), Spanish mobiles 75 to 79 (with `+34`, `0034` or none) and North American `NXX-555-01XX` numbers (`(NXX) 555-01XX` and three more layouts), all drawn from the reserved ranges so no generated number is anyone's. The fake keeps every separator and the country code, its national part is still a mobile of that country (a valid NXX area code and exchange for North America), and the text round trips. The live prefixes it used to draw (UK 071 to 075, 077 and 078, Spanish 60 to 69 and 71 to 74, exchanges other than 555) are a unit case each in `test/detect.test.ts`, allow-listed in C46. The old rule, which kept a first digit only for Spanish numbers, turns `+44 7700` into a landline and makes it fail.

## 2. The labeled corpus

`corpus/` holds synthetic emails, 336 generated by `scripts/gen-corpus.ts` and 45 handwritten, written to look like real mail: bookings, invoices, tax and social-security notices, client forwards with signatures, newsletters, security alerts, threads, and English, Spanish and mixed, plus one Hebrew booking and one Arabic invoice with known names behind glued prefixes. A survivor glued behind such a prefix (`לדוד`) counts against strict recall. Every sensitive span is annotated inline:

```
Hola, soy {{person:Ana Ruiz}} de {{org:Gestoría Norte}}. El importe es {{amount:247,80 €}}
y vence el {{date:3 de marzo}}. Llámame al {{phone:+34 791 123 456}}.
```

Everything in the corpus is invented, and the `.example` domains are reserved for that. A lint check refuses any corpus file containing a non-`.example` email domain outside the public allowlist.

`pnpm corpus` strips the annotations, runs `blind`, and scores each kind:

- **Recall:** the share of annotated values of that kind that are gone from the blind text. A value counts as gone when it no longer appears as a whole word (accents and case folded; for a person, none of its name tokens of three letters or more appears). The scorecard also prints span coverage, the share of annotations a detector span fully covered, as a diagnostic only.
- **Precision:** the share of replaced spans that were annotated. Over-replacing hurts the lab's answer, so it's measured too.
- **Round trip:** every file unblinds back to its stripped original.

The scorecard goes to `reports/corpus.json` and the evidence report.

### Recall floors

The corpus is scored twice. In `known` mode the owner's own names, orgs and places are passed as `known`, the way the CLI is meant to be used. They sit in `%known kind|value` header lines of each file and are not part of the text. In `bare` mode nothing is passed, which is the worst case. Recall is strict: an annotated value counts as found only when it is gone from the whole output, so a name that is replaced in a header and left in a greeting counts as a miss. An earlier version counted a span as found when a detector span covered it, which overstated recall (bare person recall read 29% where the value was gone from the output 4.8% of the time). Coverage is still printed, as a diagnostic. The scorecard also reports how many files with a miss are flagged by the leak check, which is the safety net for what the detectors miss. Corpus files may start with `%order mdy` for documents that write numeric dates month first.

Floors are set from a measured run, rounded down to a whole percent, and only move up. `pnpm corpus`, and so `pnpm verify`, fails if any kind drops below its floor. Switching recall from span coverage to the strict definition lowered several floors once, because the old numbers measured something easier (bare person 29 to 4, bare org 14 to 1, known person 73 to 57, known org 37 to 25, date 100 to 99; phone rose from 99 to 100). That reset was the first time a floor went down. The second, on 2026-10-24, lowered org precision from 100 to 97 (bare) and 98 (known) when sender names with company words ("Clínica Foxglove <info@...>") started to count as orgs instead of persons. The same spans had been replaced before and counted against person precision, which rose from 78 to 95 (bare) and 89 to 96 (known) in the same change. Bare org precision was 100 only because blind replaced no org at all in bare mode. What keeps it below 100 is four public senders (a tax agency, a bank alert, a newsletter, a restaurant) that the corpus leaves unannotated on purpose, the same reason email precision sits at 96. Two genuine gaps found on the way, a hotel and a clinic annotated in the body but not in the From line, were annotated. Names found once (in a header, after a title, from the known list or a detector) are also found where they appear again bare, which took bare person recall from 4 to 67 and known-mode person recall from 57 to 98. Places, and names that never appear next to a header, title or list entry, stay low until a NER detector is plugged in, and the floors say so. One floor sits at 99 for a known reason. Date: one file holds two dates two weeks apart, and the shift lands one of them on the other's real string, so the scorer sees the value survive although both were replaced. Id sat at 99 because of the dashed SSN in `handwritten/014-tax-en.txt` (known miss F1). The typed SSN recognizer catches it, so the id floor moved to 100.

## 3. The adversarial set

`corpus/adversarial/` holds inputs written to slip past, each with the expected result:

- Obfuscated addresses: `ana at gestoria dot example`, `ana(at)gestoria.example`, `ana@gestoria .example`.
- Unicode: look-alike letters (Cyrillic `а` in `Аna`), zero-width spaces inside names, full-width digits in amounts.
- Splits: a name across a line break, an IBAN with irregular spacing, a phone across two lines.
- Encodings: HTML entities (`&#64;`), quoted-printable soft breaks (`=\n`), base64 parts in a pasted MIME body.
- Words: amounts written out ("doscientos euros"), dates written out ("el tres de marzo").
- Context: names inside URLs, in email local parts, possessives ("Ana's"), and all caps.
- Round trips: one fund in capitals, as a title and in lower case, a name with and without its accent, an address in two letter cases, phones with an extension glued on, price bands one unit apart, a total one cent from an instalment, and a host with capitals and no path. These cases check `unblind(blind(x)) === x` instead of looking for secrets, and back C1. They come from the Gretel benchmark, rewritten with invented values.
- Formats: an id written as dashed digit groups (`552-83-7741`), card numbers in groups of four and in the Amex layout, IPv4 and compressed IPv6 addresses, a DNI with dots and a dash, a NIE, a CIF, EU VAT numbers, a name after a title, an id in short groups after a label, and two known misses: a name in plain prose with nothing around it, and an SSN written with spaces and no label.

Each case is either **caught**, meaning a test asserts it is replaced, or a **known miss**. A known miss has its test marked `.fails`, so CI notices when a fix starts catching it, and it gets a line in `known-misses.md` saying what slips through and why. Nothing is missed silently.

## 4. Mutation testing (Stryker)

Stryker mutates `src/`: it flips comparisons, swaps operators, deletes statements, replaces constants. It then checks that some test fails for each mutant. Coverage alone proves the code ran. A high mutation score proves the tests would notice the code being wrong. The floor starts at the first measured score, rounded down, and only moves up. Surviving mutants in `leaks.ts`, `amounts.ts` and `dates.ts` are reviewed by hand, because those files carry the safety claims.

How it runs. `vitest.mutation.config.ts` picks the suites that exercise `src/` in process, runs properties at 60 cases with a fixed seed so every mutant sees the same inputs, and skips the spawned CLI, the build checks and the network patches. `ignoreStatic` is on, because the Vitest runner cannot reload module-level code for each mutant: a mutated lookup table at the top of a file would survive whatever the tests say. Those lines are still covered by the normal suites, they are just not scored here. The word lists in `names.ts` are all static, so they drop out. The first full run took 53 minutes. With static mutants ignored a full run takes about 30 minutes, and Stryker's incremental file under `reports/mutation/` makes a rerun after a small change a few minutes. The floor is in `stryker.config.json` as `thresholds.break`. A failed `tsc` run can still write `.js` files into `src/`, and then tests and Stryker load those instead of the sources: the score collapses to a few percent and the incremental file goes wrong. A build check (B1) now fails when `src/` holds anything but TypeScript sources. After a run that looks wrong, delete `reports/mutation/incremental.json` before trusting the next one. `test/mutation-floor.test.ts` checks the report against the floor. With no report it is reported as skipped ("no mutation report; run pnpm mutation"), not as passed, unless `MUTATION_REPORT_REQUIRED` is set, for a run that must not pass without Stryker. `pnpm verify` runs Stryker itself, and its `claims --strict` step fails on a missing report.

## 5. No network (N1)

The suite runs the whole corpus and every property with these patched to throw: `fetch`, `node:http`, `node:https`, `node:net`, `node:tls`, `node:dns`, `node:dgram`, `node:child_process` and `worker_threads`. One hit fails the run. A static check also refuses any import of those modules under `src/`.

## 6. Build checks (B1)

- `package.json` has no `dependencies`, only `devDependencies`.
- `pnpm pack` contains only `dist/`, `README.md` and `LICENSE`.
- The built `dist/` imports nothing outside itself and Node built-ins, and no forbidden built-in from N1.
- B2: the library entry and everything it imports use no Node built-in and no Node-only global (`process`, `Buffer`, `require`, `__dirname`). The built entry is loaded into a fresh V8 realm through `node:vm` modules, with Web Crypto, TextEncoder, atob and btoa as the only host objects, and blinds and unblinds a text there (C19). That is what lets it run in browsers, Deno, Bun and edge runtimes.
- B3: the sentences that say what the leak check covers, `leaksOk` in `docs/PROXY.md` and in the `src/receipt.ts` doc comment and `assertNoLeaks` in the README, are pinned word for word in `test/readme.test.ts`, so an edit that claims more than found, `known` and `deny` values, or that counts an `unenforced` entry in `leaksOk`, fails. The same file checks the NER package status: every tracked Markdown file naming GLiNER or `@centrst/blind-ner` is listed either as reporting numbers measured with it or as naming it only, each reporting file says the package is a prototype built from this repository, not on npm, and every table with a GLiNER row or column and a number in it has that note in the paragraph just before or after it.
- D1: `test/deno-smoke.test.ts` spawns `deno run` on a script that imports the built `dist/index.js`, blinds and unblinds one synthetic text, and checks the round trip and that no real value survived. It skips with a message when `deno` is not on PATH, and no CI job runs it. Bun has no such test.

## 6b. Examples and receipts

`test/examples.test.ts` imports the files in `examples/` the way a builder would use them and runs each one against an in-process upstream that records what it receives. Every secret in the request must be absent from what the upstream saw, the answer must come back with real values, a request that trips the leak check must get a 422 with kinds and offsets only and must reach no upstream, and the Workers adapter's receipt must verify against the public key it publishes. `test/receipt.test.ts` signs receipts, changes every body field, the key id and the signature one at a time and expects each to fail, and checks over generated emails that no annotated value appears in a receipt. `test/examples.test.ts` also checks that the handler refuses a missing or wrong credential, an oversized body and a bad `known` entry, stops a slow upstream with a 504 and turns a throw inside `blind` or `unblind` into a 500 with none of the input, and that a caller text without the operator's `known` values gets none of them back in any body, even with `returnMapping` and an upstream that names every fake name blind draws from (C33). The receipt tests also refuse a stale receipt and one for another request id (C34), and check `canonicalJson` against the examples in RFC 8785 and a fixture receipt signed with the RFC 8032 test key (C35). B2 also signs and verifies a receipt inside the bare realm, so receipts are known to work without Node.

## 7. Benchmark against Presidio

`pnpm bench:presidio:setup` builds a Python venv with Presidio and the large spaCy models for English and Spanish, once. `pnpm bench:presidio` runs Presidio on the stripped corpus, scores it with the same strict scorer as `pnpm corpus`, runs blind on the same files, and measures how many amounts and dates each tool replaces (blind scales and shifts them) while keeping them usable for arithmetic. The results, versions and caveats are in [scripts/bench/RESULTS.md](scripts/bench/RESULTS.md). It is informational, not a gate: the corpus is blind's home ground, and Presidio's misses there say less than its wins. Spans Presidio finds that blind misses are candidates for new corpus or adversarial cases.

`pnpm bench:llmguard` adds LLM Guard's Anonymize scanner to the same tables, and `pnpm bench:gretel` scores all three tools on a sample of a dataset blind's authors did not write. Results and caveats for both are in [scripts/bench/RESULTS-llmguard-gretel.md](scripts/bench/RESULTS-llmguard-gretel.md).

## 8. The evidence report

`pnpm evidence` writes `reports/evidence.html`, one page holding:

- the claims table with pass or fail per claim,
- recall and precision per kind, against the floors,
- the mutation score and surviving mutants in the safety files,
- the known-miss list,
- ten side-by-side corpus examples (real and blind are both synthetic),
- the exact commit, seed and command that produced it.

Every release attaches the report. Anyone who asks "what does it actually do" gets this page.

## 9. The optional NER detector

Names, orgs and places are the weak spot of a pattern engine. `ner/` is a separate workspace package, `@centrst/blind-ner`, that adds them through the `detectors` option. The core is untouched: still no runtime dependencies (C8), still no network or process calls (C7). The package is where the dependencies live (`onnxruntime-node`, `@huggingface/tokenizers`). It is a prototype built from this repository, not on npm, so the NER numbers in this section and in the benchmark reports come from that prototype.

**Model.** `urchade/gliner_multi-v2.1` in the ONNX export from `onnx-community`, fp32. It is a multilingual mDeBERTa-v3 GLiNER, Apache 2.0 upstream, and takes the labels as a prompt, so `person`, `organization` and `location` need no fine-tuning. `gliner_multi_pii-v1` was tried too and scored lower on all three kinds (bare person 31% against 33%, place 87% against 96% before propagation, same round trips), because it is tuned for PII labels rather than names in prose. [ner/CANDIDATES.md](ner/CANDIDATES.md) compares it, `gliner-multi-pii-domains-v1` and `knowledgator/gliner-pii-base-v1.0` with the baseline on the corpus and on a Gretel sample, with a threshold sweep. None is better on both. The int8 export is not usable: its scores collapse (best logit about -1.8 against +4.1 in fp32) and it finds nothing. The weights are 1.1 GB and are fetched once with `pnpm ner:download`, which pins a revision and checks SHA-256. The detector never imports that script. Inference is fp32 on the CPU and has been bit-identical across runs on one machine. N8 proves the pipeline is deterministic with a stub backend, not that ONNX Runtime is identical across machines or thread counts, so `threads` is there to pin it when that matters.

**In process, not a server.** A loopback model server would add a port, a second process to start and stop, and a claim C7 could not make about the package. In-process ONNX Runtime keeps one process and no socket, and the no-network test (C16) can patch every network and process module and still run inference. The cost is a native addon and 1.1 GB of resident weights in the caller. The `gliner` npm package was tried first and dropped: its word splitter uses an ASCII `\w`, so "Gestoría" splits into three words, and it drags in a tokenizer stack with `sharp` and two ONNX Runtime copies. `ner/src/gliner.ts` is a runner of about 175 lines for the span model with a Unicode splitter.

**Async and sync.** `blind` finds spans synchronously and a model call is async. `createNerDetector(model).prepare(texts)` runs the model once per text, then returns an ordinary `Detector` that replays the result. `blindWithNer(model, texts, opts)` wraps both steps. Detection runs on the same-length look-alike fold, so the model sees the folded text and the cache is keyed by it. Invisible characters are removed before the model sees the text and the offsets are mapped back.

**Merging.** NER spans enter at priority 50, below every pattern detector (60 to 100) and below `known` (70), and `resolveSpans` does the rest. Before that, each NER span is cut around the structured spans (email, url, iban, phone, id, amount, date) so a name that touches an address keeps its name part. A leading greeting or title ("Estimada", "Dear", "Dr") is trimmed. A name found by the model or by a header line is also marked wherever it appears bare later in the same text, as long as the text never writes that word in lower case outside an address. That step is worth most of the person recall, because a model rarely tags "Pilar" after "Buenos días".

**Evidence.** `pnpm test:ner` runs the stub tests (always) and the model tests (when the weights are present and onnxruntime-node's binary loads on the platform, else they skip and C15 to C18 show as skipped in CLAIMS.md; `pnpm verify` runs the claims check in strict mode and needs the model). `pnpm ner:eval` prints the before and after scorecard, the English and Spanish split and the adversarial comparison. `corpus/floors-ner.json` holds the floors for the detector, set from the first run and rounded down. Threshold 0.3 is the default. A sweep over 0.5, 0.3, 0.15 and 0.1 raised recall at every step and moved precision by one to two points, so the default is a judgment about unseen mail, not a corpus fit.

The corpus is synthetic and its names are uncommon, which a model finds easier than the Garcías and Smiths of real mail. Treat the NER numbers as a ceiling, not a forecast.
